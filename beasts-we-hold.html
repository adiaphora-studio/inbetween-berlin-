01 — THE HANDRAIL

The handrail is part of the architecture.

We usually notice a staircase because it takes us somewhere. The handrail is different. It is designed to be touched, followed and used almost without conscious attention.

In Berlin's older residential buildings, however, the handrail can be much more than a functional object. Wood, iron, stone, glass, stucco and painted surfaces were often brought together as parts of a larger interior. The staircase was designed as a space in its own right.

02 — HISTORICISM

Old forms, new buildings.

Much of the architecture associated with Berlin's late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries is connected to Historicism. Rather than developing one single new style, architects and craftsmen repeatedly returned to earlier periods and adapted their forms for contemporary buildings.

Romanesque, Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque and Rococo elements could appear separately or be combined within the same building. During the Gründerzeit, this approach became particularly visible in Berlin's rapidly expanding residential districts.

Ornament was therefore not simply an addition placed onto an otherwise finished building. It could help establish an atmosphere, distinguish an entrance or staircase, and contribute to the overall impression of a house.

03 — THE STAIRCASE AS ENSEMBLE

A staircase was rarely just a staircase.

In many Berlin Altbauten, the staircase belongs to a larger decorative ensemble. Patterned floors, coloured glass, doors, wall surfaces, stucco, ironwork and painted decoration could all contribute to the same visual language.

The handrail sits directly inside this ensemble. It follows the movement of the staircase while also carrying its own ornament. A curve can become a neck, a carved end can suggest a head, and a small detail that would be almost invisible elsewhere suddenly becomes something that the hand encounters every day.

These interiors also tell us something about the social ambitions of their period. Decorative architecture could communicate taste, refinement and a connection to established historical forms. It could contribute to the representation of a household and to the character of a building without necessarily being designed as a literal status symbol.

04 — ANIMALS

Lions, dolphins and other creatures.

Animal motifs had a long history in European decorative art. Lions could evoke strength, guardianship or heraldic traditions. Dolphins belonged to a different but equally established repertoire of classical and ornamental imagery.

In the staircase, however, these figures do not necessarily need to carry one fixed symbolic meaning. They can simply be part of an ornamental vocabulary — selected, adapted and combined with other forms to make an interior distinctive.

That ambiguity is part of what makes these details interesting today. A creature that once belonged to a larger decorative system can survive as a fragment of everyday architecture.

05 — WHO MADE THEM?

Most of the time, the maker is harder to find.

The history of a building is not necessarily the history of every person who worked on it. Architectural records may preserve the name of an architect, developer or owner while individual decorative elements remain undocumented.

This does not mean that the craftsmen behind them were unimportant. Quite the opposite: these details depended on carpentry, carving, metalwork, stucco, glass and other forms of specialised labour.

Archival survival is uneven. Some interiors have documented artists or sculptors; many individual handrails and ornaments are simply recorded as parts of a building. Looking closely at them therefore means looking at a layer of architectural history that is often easier to see than to name.
